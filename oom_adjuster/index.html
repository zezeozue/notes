How Android decides which processes to kill, slow down or freeze

Android keeps the apps you have left in memory so that switching back is fast. Memory and CPU are limited, so something has to rank every process. In system_server that is OomAdjuster. After every activity change, binding or broadcast it re-ranks the affected processes and updates the low-memory killer, the kernel and the app. This note explains how it computes those ranks, how the results are applied, and how to read every transition in a Perfetto trace.

Checked against AOSP as of Android 17 (frameworks/base, system/memory/lmkd, system/core/libprocessgroup, external/perfetto).

At a glance

  • What it decides. Four outputs per process: how soon it may be killed (oom_score_adj), what lifecycle role it is in (procState), how much CPU it gets (schedGroup), and which capabilities it holds, including whether it may be frozen.
  • How it decides. A process is at least as important as the most important client bound to it, so importance flows along service bindings and content-provider connections.
  • Why kill-order scores move when nothing visible changes. Each output is kept in raw, effective, committed and reported copies. Local adjustments for recency ladders and short service callbacks can move oom_score_adj without changing procState or CPU group.
  • It is not the only writer. Zygote at fork, SchedulingPolicyService for media threads, and CachedAppOptimizer's direct thaw paths change CPU groups and freeze state outside OomAdjuster.
  • How to observe it. A Perfetto trace records every oom_score_adj write, the updateOomAdj_* slice that caused it, and every freeze and thaw interval with its unfreeze reason.

Terms.

  • Client and host: a process that binds to a service or queries a content provider is the client; the process providing that service or content provider is the host.
  • Cached process: a live background app process with no visible UI or active components, kept in RAM so a later launch skips cold startup.
  • LRU list: the least-recently-used list of running app processes kept by ActivityManagerService (AMS), ordered from least to most recently active.
  • Pass: one execution of OomAdjuster (a full update across the LRU list or a partial update over a subgraph) that recomputes and commits process state.
  • adj (oom_score_adj): the per-process kill-order score (-1000 to 1000), where higher scores are killed earlier by lmkd (the user-space low-memory killer daemon).
  • cgroup and cpuset: Linux control groups that cap which CPU cores a task may run on (cpuset), its scheduler weight, and whether its threads are frozen.
  • Frozen process and thaw: a process placed into the cgroup v2 freezer (cgroup.freeze = 1) so its threads receive no CPU time, and the unfreeze (thaw) that lets it run again.
Part I · What it decides

1OomAdjuster turns what each app is doing into four numbers

A phone runs far more processes than it has RAM to keep forever, so something must decide which processes to reclaim first. Android does not exit an app's process when you leave its screen: the process stays cached so returning to it skips cold startup. When free memory drops, killing the wrong process is immediately noticeable — background music stops, or the input method restarts.

The process that sees every app's components is system_server, through ActivityManagerService (AMS) and its com.android.server.am.psc (ProcessStateController) package. AMS tracks which activity is on screen, which process runs a foreground service, which is receiving a broadcast, and which processes are connected through bindings or content providers. OomAdjuster turns that component graph into per-process numbers that daemons and the kernel enforce. Its name comes from the Linux out-of-memory (OOM) killer, whose /proc/<pid>/oom_score_adj knob it originally drove.

Each managed process receives four outputs, whose scales are detailed in §2:

OutputWhat it isWho acts on it
oom_score_adjAn integer from -1000 to 1000, where higher means killed earlier. Called adj in AMS code.lmkd, the user-space low-memory killer daemon, which receives it over a control socket, writes /proc/<pid>/oom_score_adj, and kills from the highest score down under memory pressure. The kernel OOM killer reads the same file as a last resort.
procStateOne of the ActivityManager.PROCESS_STATE_* lifecycle states (0 to 20), where lower is more important.The app itself (via IApplicationThread.setProcessState), per-UID state published to system services (UidRecord), and the capability rules below.
schedGroupA CPU scheduling class: background, restricted, default, top app, top-app bound, or foreground window.The kernel, through cgroups and task profiles applied by Process.setProcessGroup, plus UI and RenderThread priority boosts for the top app.
capabilityA bitmask of ActivityManager.PROCESS_CAPABILITY_* flags: foreground location, camera, microphone, network access, background foreground-service launch (BFSL), and CPU time.Permission and network policy checks, plus the cached-app freezer, which checks the two CPU-time bits to decide whether a process may be frozen.

Importance is not a property of one process in isolation. When the top app binds to a service in another process or queries a content provider, the host process must stay alive and scheduled too, or the top app stalls waiting on Binder. OomAdjuster therefore computes all four outputs over a directed graph of processes, with an edge from each client to each host, including graphs with cycles.

What it means for you When a process stays unexpectedly protected or gets killed or frozen, look at the connection graph: who was bound to it, with which BIND_* flags, and what those clients' own scores were at that moment.

The CPU group runs in the opposite direction: larger numbers grant more CPU, starting at background and rising to top app and top-app bound. It uses OomAdjuster's own SCHED_GROUP_* constants rather than kernel thread-group IDs; §8 explains how the two scales map.

Capability bits are defined on ActivityManager. Two bits govern the freezer: PROCESS_CAPABILITY_CPU_TIME means the process has an explicit reason to execute and must not be frozen, while PROCESS_CAPABILITY_IMPLICIT_CPU_TIME keeps a process unfrozen because its adj sits below the freezer cutoff. A third bit, PROCESS_CAPABILITY_BFSL, permits starting a foreground service from the background.

What it means for you Read adj for "who dies first" and procState for "what the framework thinks the process is doing". A process in the visible adj band with procState BOUND_FOREGROUND_SERVICE is protected because of a binding, not because it owns a visible window.

3Updates run on specific events and touch only what can change

Recomputing every running process is expensive, so AMS triggers an update only at component transitions and scopes each pass to the processes whose scores can move. Every pass runs in system_server under the AMS lock, and an app switch fires many transitions in quick succession. Running too broadly wastes CPU under lock; running too rarely leaves stale scores in lmkd.

Callers enter through ProcessStateController, or through ActivityManagerService.updateOomAdjLocked, which delegates to the same OomAdjuster instance. Every call passes an OOM_ADJ_REASON_* code from ActivityManagerInternal.java identifying the trigger — such as an activity visibility change, broadcast delivery, service bind, content-provider acquisition, process death, or a timed follow-up (Numbers and method).

  • Full update (runFullUpdate → fullUpdateLSP): recomputes every process on the AMS LRU list.
  • Partial update (runUpdate(proc), or enqueueUpdateTarget followed by runPendingUpdate → partialUpdateLSP): recomputes only the target processes and every host reachable from them along outgoing service and provider connections. Unreached clients keep their committed values and act as fixed inputs.
  • Follow-up update (OOM_ADJ_REASON_FOLLOW_UP): scheduled on mUpdateHandler via maybeSetProcessFollowUpUpdateLocked when a timed state expires — such as the content-provider retention window, the top-to-foreground-service grace period, or the previous-app timeout.

An update requested re-entrantly while a pass is already running does not nest. Instead, OomAdjuster marks mPendingFullOomAdjUpdate or adds targets to mPendingProcessSet and drains them when the active pass finishes. Batch sessions on ProcessStateController similarly coalesce multiple component edits into a single pass at session close, and single connection changes run a fast pre-check before starting a pass at all (§6, Case 4).

What it means for you Each pass emits an atrace slice named updateOomAdj_<reason> on a system_server thread (from oomAdjReasonToString). The suffix tells you which AMS event triggered a score transition (§12).
Part II · How scores are computed

4Each update runs in five ordered steps

A single linear scan over running processes cannot score them accurately, so each pass divides the work into five ordered steps. Two dependencies force that structure: a host's score depends on clients that may sit anywhere in the graph (including cycles), and recency ladders depend on each process's final band across the whole LRU list. Applying LRU offsets during graph propagation would compound the offset at every hop along a binding chain.

OomAdjusterImpl orchestrates the pass across every process in fullUpdateLSP, or across the target subgraph in partialUpdateLSP. The LSP suffix indicates that the caller holds both mServiceLock and mProcLock.

Step by step, as fullUpdateLSP and partialUpdateLSP run them:

  1. Intrinsic state. Step 1 clears per-pass activity memos (resetCachedInfo()) and scores each process from its own components alone (computeOomAdjLSP). A process with nothing active receives UNKNOWN_ADJ and PROCESS_STATE_CACHED_EMPTY as placeholders, stores its raw and intermediate values (§5), and enters the propagation queues.
  2. Propagation. computeConnectionsLSP drains two priority queues so client scores propagate along service and provider bindings.
  3. Shadow graph controllers. Behind enable_procstate_controller_computation and enable_capability_controller_computation, ProcStateController and CapabilityController evaluate explicit ProcessNode and GraphEdge objects (via partialUpdateProcessGraphLSP on partial passes). In Android 17 both run computation-only without overwriting the live outputs from steps 1 and 2.
  4. LRU post-pass. applyLruAdjust walks the LRU list from most to least recent (skipped on a partial update when no reached process requires laddering or a cached slot). Processes in the visible and previous bands receive consecutive scores, and processes still at UNKNOWN_ADJ receive cached or empty slots across 900–999.
  5. Apply and trim. postUpdateOomAdjInnerLSP calls updateAndTrimProcessLSP, which invokes applyResultsLSP per process (Part III) and trims cached and empty processes when their counts exceed configured limits.

Reading the method names

Method names in OomAdjuster reflect lock discipline or historical structure rather than pipeline stage. Three naming conventions explain almost all of them:

  • Suffixes name locks, not stages. …Locked requires mServiceLock, and …LSP requires both mServiceLock and mProcLock, matching @GuardedBy({"mServiceLock", "mProcLock"}).
  • "Compute" means intrinsic or single-edge scoring. computeOomAdjLSP scores only a process's own components in step 1; the score is not final until propagation and applyLruAdjust finish.
  • "Apply" is used for both step 4 and step 5. applyLruAdjust mutates in-flight scores in step 4, whereas applyOomAdjLSP and applyResultsLSP publish committed changes in step 5.

Scores flow from clients to hosts, most important first

Step 2 always pops the most important pending process next, allowing a host's propagated score to settle without redundant relaxation. Outgoing edges come from forEachConnectionLSP: active bindService connections, SDK-sandbox bindings, and content-provider connections into other processes. For each edge, computeServiceHostOomAdjLSP or computeProviderHostOomAdjLSP evaluates the host from the client's raw fields, getCurRawAdj() and getCurRawProcState().

The two priority queues are bucketed linked lists: mProcessRecordProcStateNodes indexed by curProcState, and mProcessRecordAdjNodes indexed by curRawAdj. Popping the best bucket first mirrors Dijkstra's order — once a process is popped, no worse-scored node remaining in the queue can improve it further.

  • Pass 1 pops every queued process in curProcState order and evaluates all of its outgoing connections. Any host whose curProcState or curRawAdj improves is re-queued.
  • Pass 2 pops, in curRawAdj order, only processes whose raw adj improved during pass 1 and re-evaluates their outgoing edges until no host improves.

Binding cycles terminate cleanly because a host re-enters a queue only when one of its values strictly improves, and every scale has a fixed ceiling of importance. A cycle can never lift any member above the best external score entering the cycle.

Once the last client disconnects from a content provider, how quickly the host drops back to its own score depends on how the provider was acquired (Figure 6). An external provider handle (getContentProviderExternal, tracked via hasExternalProcessHandles) lifts the host to FOREGROUND_APP_ADJ (adjType = "ext-provider") while open, but records no retention timestamp when closed.

What it means for you To inspect why a live process holds a given score, run adb shell dumpsys activity oom. Each entry prints adjType (the rule that won, such as top-activity, service or provider) and, for propagated scores, the exact adjTarget component and adjSource client process.

5Each score is kept in several copies, one per stage

Different steps of the pipeline need different views of the same process, so ProcessRecordInternal stores multiple copies of each output. Propagation needs a client's score before local per-process penalties are applied; the LRU post-pass needs to ladder scores without feeding those offsets back into bindings; and commit needs the previously sent values so it writes out only changes. Field names combine two axes: version (raw vs effective) and visibility (in-flight vs committed vs reported).

Why keep both setAdj and setRawAdj

They answer different questions. setAdj is the deduplication baseline for lmkd: if the newly computed effective score matches setAdj, step 5 sends no socket packet. setRawAdj preserves the pre-adjustment score so a restarted process can be seeded before local adjustments run again.

Neither field can be reconstructed from the other because local adjustments are many-to-one:

Why max and cached exist

The ceiling field, mMaxAdj, fixes a process's worst-case protection when the process starts, regardless of what the binding graph later does. Centralising that guarantee in one clamp inside setIntermediateAdjLSP avoids scattering special cases for system_server, persistent apps and isolated system services across every scoring branch, and lets computeOomAdjLSP return immediately when mMaxAdj <= FOREGROUND_APP_ADJ.

The mCachedAdj, mCachedProcState and mCachedSchedGroup fields avoid repeated window-manager walks. Scoring activities queries WindowProcessController to inspect visible, pausing and stopping activities across tasks. Because step 2 can revisit a process multiple times during propagation, the first lookup is memoized on the process record and cleared by resetCachedInfo() at the start of the next pass.

Two meanings of "cached" mCached* fields on ProcessRecordInternal are a per-pass memo of an activity scan. A cached app is an idle background process in the 900–999 band. The two concepts are unrelated.

6Four cases where the copies differ, and why that matters

For a standalone foreground app with no special bindings, all four adj copies finish the pass with the same number. The four cases below show where raw, effective and committed copies diverge — and why collapsing them into one field would break either binding propagation or update skipping.

Case 1 · A binding that asks to rank the host above the client

A client binding with Context.BIND_ABOVE_CLIENT demotes only its own effective score, never the raw score it passes to other services. When app A binds service B with BIND_ABOVE_CLIENT, it asks lmkd to reclaim A before B. OomAdjuster implements that in three parts:

  • When the binding is created, A's ProcessServiceRecordInternal.updateHasAboveClient() sets mHasAboveClient = true whenever at least one outgoing binding carries BIND_ABOVE_CLIENT to another process.
  • In step 2, computeServiceHostOomAdjLSP lets B inherit A's raw score without the usual visible-band floor. If A is the top app, B receives the foreground score.
  • Whenever A's raw score is written, setIntermediateAdjLSP calls applyBindAboveClientToAdj, stepping A's curAdj one band down (for example from foreground to visible) while leaving A's curRawAdj untouched.

Now suppose A also holds an ordinary binding to a sibling service C (Figure 9, left). Because step 2 reads A.getCurRawAdj(), C still sees A as a top app rather than inheriting A's self-demotion. The mMaxAdj clamp and the zram-writeback cap work the same way: both modify curAdj without altering curRawAdj.

Case 2 · The visible ladder renumbers every member of the visible band

When both oomadjuster_vis_laddering and remove_lru_spam_prevention are enabled, applyLruAdjust spreads every process in the visible band across consecutive integers by recency so lmkd reclaims older ones first. Both apps with visible activities (such as split-screen or picture-in-picture windows) and headless service hosts bound by top or visible apps sit in this band; when either flag is off, ComputeOomAdjWindowCallback offsets visible activities by window taskLayer while headless bound hosts share the base score.

Writing only curAdj prevents ladder drift down binding chains. If host S binds a second host T, propagation gives T the band's base score from S.getCurRawAdj(), and the LRU post-pass then assigns T a ladder slot solely from its own LRU position. Because headless hosts also sit on the ladder, any shift in LRU order above them renumbers their curAdj and writes lmkd even when their procState and capabilities stay identical.

Case 3 · A short service callback temporarily pins adj to foreground

While a service lifecycle callback (onCreate, onBind, onStartCommand, onUnbind or onDestroy) is in flight on the main thread, hasExecutingServices() remains true until serviceDoneExecutingLocked runs. During that window, step 1 grants the host FOREGROUND_APP_ADJ and PROCESS_STATE_SERVICE, with SCHED_GROUP_DEFAULT for foreground execution and SCHED_GROUP_BACKGROUND otherwise, protecting the callback from being killed or starved before it finishes.

When the host is already bound by an important client — for example, bound by the top app with BOUND_TOP in the visible band — the intrinsic and propagation steps split the outputs (Figure 10). Propagation cannot improve on the intrinsic foreground adj, yet it restores the stronger BOUND_TOP procState and capabilities from the top-app binding. Only oom_score_adj moves: down to 0 when the callback starts and back to the visible score when serviceDoneExecutingLocked completes.

Case 4 · Some binding changes skip the update entirely

A less-important client cannot be the reason an already-important host is elevated, so AMS skips running a pass when adding or removing a single connection cannot change the host. Before scheduling a partial update on bind or unbind, AMS calls evaluateServiceConnectionAdd or evaluateServiceConnectionRemoval (or the content-provider equivalents), where true means an update is needed. Because no pass is active yet, both methods inspect the committed set* fields:

psc/OomAdjuster.java · evaluateServiceConnectionRemovalpublic boolean evaluateServiceConnectionRemoval(ProcessRecordInternal client,
        ProcessRecordInternal app, ConnectionRecordInternal cr) {
    if (evaluateConnectionPrelude(client, app)) {
        return true;  // null, isolated, SDK sandbox or killed: always update
    }

    if (app.getSetAdj() >= client.getSetAdj()) {
        return true;
    } else if (app.getSetProcState() >= client.getSetProcState()) {
        return true;
    } else if (cr.hasFlag(Context.BIND_INCLUDE_CAPABILITIES
                        | Context.BIND_BYPASS_USER_NETWORK_RESTRICTIONS)
            && (app.getSetCapability() & client.getSetCapability())
                        != PROCESS_CAPABILITY_NONE) {
        return true;
    } else if ((client.getSetCapability() & app.getSetCapability()
                & ALL_CPU_TIME_CAPABILITIES) != 0) {
        return true;
    }
    return false;
}

When the host is strictly better than the client in both setAdj and setProcState and shares none of the capabilities the connection could transmit, that client could not have been what held the host up, so removing the edge changes nothing. Notice how Case 2 interacts with this test: on the visible ladder, a host that happens to be more recent in the LRU list than its visible client can have a slightly lower setAdj than that client, so checking setAdj alone would falsely skip the unbind. Checking setProcState alongside setAdj catches it, because a propagated binding never gives a host a strictly lower procState than the client that lifted it.

Adding a connection runs the mirror pre-check in evaluateServiceConnectionAdd: if the client is better than the host in adj, procState, or a transmissible capability, it calls computeServiceHostOomAdjLSP(cr, app, client, now, true /* dryRun */) to test that single edge without mutating state, and skips the partial pass whenever nothing would improve.

8OomAdjuster ranks CPU groups on its own scale

OomAdjuster defines its own SCHED_GROUP_* constants in psc/Constants.java rather than storing android.os.Process.THREAD_GROUP_* constants directly. Two requirements drive that separation: comparing groups as integers during binding propagation, and distinguishing the top app from services bound to it.

Reason 1 · The kernel's group IDs are not in priority order

Step 2 combines CPU scheduling groups along bindings by keeping the larger integer, which works only when higher numbers mean stronger CPU access. A service host may have several clients bound to it, and its group must settle at the best grant among them. Rather than consulting a priority lookup table at every edge, OomAdjusterImpl compares scheduling groups with > (Figure 12):

psc/OomAdjusterImpl.java · computeServiceHostOomAdjLSPfinal int curSchedGroup = client.getCurrentSchedulingGroup();
if (curSchedGroup > schedGroup) {
    if (cr.hasFlag(Context.BIND_IMPORTANT)) {
        schedGroup = curSchedGroup;
    } else {
        schedGroup = SCHED_GROUP_DEFAULT;
    }
}

The kernel-facing constants in Process.java are historical identifiers, not ranks: compared numerically, THREAD_GROUP_RESTRICTED would outrank THREAD_GROUP_TOP_APP, and THREAD_GROUP_BACKGROUND would outrank THREAD_GROUP_DEFAULT. In psc/Constants.java, the core groups from background through top-app bound rise monotonically from 0 to 4.

One historical quirk remains: SCHED_GROUP_FOREGROUND_WINDOW, added later for visible non-top windows (whose comment notes its priority sits between default and top app), has value 5, above SCHED_GROUP_TOP_APP (3). Ordinary bindings still cap propagated groups at SCHED_GROUP_DEFAULT unless BIND_IMPORTANT is set.

Reason 2 · Top app and top-app bound share CPUs but not the UI boost

Some bound service hosts execute work on the critical path of the visible UI, so confining them to slow background cores would stall frames. Giving them the top-app cpuset lets them run on big cores, yet they are not rendering the user's active window themselves and should not receive the top app's UI-thread boost (THREAD_PRIORITY_TOP_APP_BOOST or SCHED_FIFO) or RenderThread boost.

SCHED_GROUP_TOP_APP_BOUND expresses "top-app cpuset without UI-thread boost" (Figure 13). A service host receives it when bound from a visible activity with both BIND_ADJUST_WITH_ACTIVITY and BIND_IMPORTANT (whereas a BIND_IMPORTANT binding directly from the top app, or BIND_SCHEDULE_LIKE_TOP_APP from the system, grants full SCHED_GROUP_TOP_APP). In step 5, applyResultsLSP collapses the two for cgroups while keeping them separate for thread priority:

  • The switch (curSchedGroup) block maps both SCHED_GROUP_TOP_APP and SCHED_GROUP_TOP_APP_BOUND to Process.THREAD_GROUP_TOP_APP.
  • The UI-thread and RenderThread priority boost runs only when curSchedGroup == SCHED_GROUP_TOP_APP.

9Other code also changes CPU groups and thread priorities

OomAdjuster chooses each managed process's baseline scheduling group, but three other paths write cgroups or per-thread scheduler policies independently. Knowing those writers explains traces where a thread's actual CPU group or FIFO priority differs from mSetSchedGroup:

  1. Zygote at process fork. Without a launch hint, a cold-started top activity would spend its initial milliseconds in the default cgroup before OomAdjuster runs its first pass. When launching for the top activity, ProcessList.startProcess passes --is-top-app (Zygote.START_AS_TOP_APP_ARG) so native fork code in com_android_internal_os_Zygote.cpp calls SetSchedulerPolicy to place the child straight into SP_TOP_APP. When the app attaches, setAttachingProcessStatesLSP boosts its UI thread and, while the screen is on or animating, holds top-app state into the first pass.
  2. SchedulingPolicyService for audio and media. Media and audio servers call requestPriority on SchedulingPolicyService to promote an individual thread — such as an app's audio callback thread — into THREAD_GROUP_RT_APP or THREAD_GROUP_AUDIO_SYS with SCHED_FIFO real-time priority. Its requestCpusetBoost path also moves the native media.codec process into THREAD_GROUP_TOP_APP during active decoding and restores it afterward.
  3. Deferred handler and phantom-process mirroring. As noted in §7, step 5 dispatches setProcessGroup asynchronously on a handler thread, and ActivityManagerService.onProcessGroupUpdated mirrors that group onto any forked native child processes tracked by PhantomProcessList.
Caveat · mSetSchedGroup vs kernel reality mSetSchedGroup records only the process-wide group OomAdjuster last requested. An audio thread promoted by SchedulingPolicyService can run in SCHED_FIFO inside a real-time cgroup while its owning process sits in SCHED_GROUP_BACKGROUND, and the process cgroup itself trails mSetSchedGroup until the handler message executes.
Part IV · Process freezer

10A process is frozen once nothing gives it a reason to run

OomAdjuster permits freezing a process as soon as the process holds neither CPU-time capability bit. Left unfrozen, a cached app is invisible to the user yet far from idle: periodic timers, handler messages and stray Binder work keep waking it up. To stop that background CPU drain, CachedAppOptimizer freezes cached processes in two steps: first freezeBinder blocks incoming Binder transactions on the process's kernel Binder interface (failing if transactions are currently in flight), and then Process.setProcessFrozen applies the libprocessgroup Frozen task profile by writing 1 to cgroup v2 cgroup.freeze.

Whether a process is eligible to be frozen is decided in psc/OomAdjuster.java from its capability mask:

psc/OomAdjuster.javapublic static final int ALL_CPU_TIME_CAPABILITIES =
        PROCESS_CAPABILITY_CPU_TIME | PROCESS_CAPABILITY_IMPLICIT_CPU_TIME;
…
public static boolean getFreezePolicy(ProcessRecordInternal proc) {
    if ((proc.getCurCapability() & ALL_CPU_TIME_CAPABILITIES) != 0) {
        return false;
    }
    // Default, freeze a process.
    return true;
}

Step 1 computes both CPU-time bits via getCpuCapability and getImplicitCpuCapability, and step 2 propagates them across connections:

  • PROCESS_CAPABILITY_CPU_TIME (explicit reasons in getCpuTimeReasons): granted when the UID is on a power allowlist, procState is TOP or better, or the process has foreground activities, an executing service callback, a running foreground service, an active broadcast receiver, or active instrumentation. (In getDefaultCapability, explicit_cpu_capability_for_top_processes strips the CPU-time bits from PROCESS_CAPABILITY_ALL so top and persistent processes obtain CPU time solely through this explicit path.)
  • PROCESS_CAPABILITY_IMPLICIT_CPU_TIME (score-based in getImplicitCpuCapability): granted whenever the process's curAdj or maxAdj sits strictly below mFreezerCutoffAdj. In ActivityManagerConstants, freezer_cutoff_adj defaults to CACHED_APP_MIN_ADJ (900), or to HOME_APP_ADJ (600) when prototype_aggressive_freezing is enabled.
  • Propagation across bindings (getCpuCapabilitiesFromClient): every service and provider connection forwards the client's CPU-time bits to the host, except a service binding created with Context.BIND_ALLOW_FREEZE when no synchronous Binder call on that connection is active.

Freezing the instant the last CPU-time bit clears would thrash processes that receive back-to-back broadcasts or short provider queries. CachedAppOptimizer therefore applies a debounce window, mFreezerDebounceTimeout (config_defaultFreezerDebounceTimeout, 10 seconds by default and configurable via DeviceConfig freezer_debounce_timeout). When the commit step calls updateAppFreezeStateLSP and getFreezePolicy returns true, freezeAppAsyncLSP posts SET_FROZEN_PROCESS_MSG on FreezeHandler delayed until the debounce window elapses; if another reason to run arrives before then, unfreezeAppLSP cancels the pending message (Figure 14).

Notice the asymmetry in step 5 when onProcessFreezabilityChanged runs. Transitioning to "do not freeze" calls unfreezeAppLSP synchronously inside the pass so the target can receive Binder calls immediately. Transitioning to "freeze" calls freezeAppAsyncLSP, leaving the actual freeze to run asynchronously on the freezer handler thread.

What it means for you To stay unfrozen in the background, a process needs an explicit CPU-time reason (foreground service, active receiver, power allowlist), an adj below mFreezerCutoffAdj, or an active client bound without BIND_ALLOW_FREEZE. Adding BIND_ALLOW_FREEZE lets a client keep a service host alive in memory while still allowing the freezer to stop its CPU use when idle.

11Many thaws and freezes bypass OomAdjuster

A frozen cached process occasionally needs to run briefly — to receive a broadcast, publish a content provider, or release a kernel file lock — without changing its overall importance. Running a full OomAdjuster pass for every such blip would waste lock time in system_server, so AMS and CachedAppOptimizer thaw and re-queue freezes directly without touching oom_score_adj:

How each path operates in CachedAppOptimizer.java and ActivityManagerService.java:

  1. The queued freeze. freezeAppAsyncLSP posts SET_FROZEN_PROCESS_MSG to FreezeHandler delayed by mFreezerDebounceTimeout (never earlier than the process's earliest freezable timestamp). When the message fires, freezeProcess freezes Binder and writes cgroup.freeze, and can also enqueue memory compaction (compactApp) for the newly frozen cached process.
  2. unfreezeTemporarily. ActivityManagerService.unfreezeTemporarily collects the target process plus every host reachable from it along bindings, and calls CachedAppOptimizer.unfreezeTemporarily on each. That thaws the process immediately and queues a fresh freeze delayed by at least mFreezerDebounceTimeout. Broadcast delivery (BroadcastQueueImpl), content-provider installation (ContentProviderHelper), and AMS process pings all use this path.
  3. Pending Binder transactions at freeze time. If freezeBinder fails because transactions are still in flight, handleBinderFreezerFailure thaws the process with UNFREEZE_REASON_BINDER_TXNS and schedules a retry with a shrinking, randomised timeout. Once the retry window drops below its minimum threshold, the process is killed rather than retried forever.
  4. Binder transactions arriving while frozen. Incoming calls to an already-frozen target do not thaw it. Instead, when the caller receives a Binder error, binderError schedules an inspection of frozen processes: a target that received a synchronous call while frozen is killed (SUBREASON_FREEZER_BINDER_TRANSACTION), as is one whose asynchronous Binder buffer is running out of space.
  5. Kernel file locks. After freezing a process, FreezeHandler runs a deadlock check over /proc/locks via ProcLocksReader. If a frozen process holds a file lock that blocks an unfreezable process, onBlockingFileLock thaws the holder with UNFREEZE_REASON_FILE_LOCKS.
  6. Shell and heap-dump overrides. adb shell am freeze [--sticky] <process> forces a freeze via forceFreezeAppAsyncLSP, and am unfreeze thaws with reason NONE. Calling enableFreezer(false) — used around dumpHeap and enableAppFreezer — thaws every frozen process with UNFREEZE_REASON_FEATURE_FLAGS until re-enabled.
What it means for you Seeing a cached process thaw and re-freeze in a trace while its oom_score_adj stays flat in the 900–999 band is normal. Inspect the unfreeze reason (§12): start_receiver, finish_receiver, get_provider, ping, binder_txns or file_locks indicate one of the direct paths above rather than an OomAdjuster score change.
